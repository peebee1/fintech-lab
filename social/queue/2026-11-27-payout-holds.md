Paying sellers the day after the sale, with no reserve, is cheaper than holding their
money for two weeks.

I built a book of 400 marketplace sellers, 180 days, 52,349 orders and USD 6,927,844.81
of GMV. Refunds cost USD 254,124.66 and arrive between 2 and 120 days after the sale.
Pay T+1 with no reserve and USD 57,605.04 of that comes back as a write-off, 22.7
percent of the refund book.

A 15 percent reserve held 30 days cuts the write-off to USD 21,835.52 for USD 12,329.06
of float. A 14 day hold on every seller writes off more, USD 33,537.86, and costs 2.6
times the float, USD 31,813.71. Every hold rung I tried, including three days, is worse
in total than paying next day, because a hold taxes 100 percent of sales and a refund
touches 3.7 percent of them.

The clever answer does not rescue it. Banding new and risky sellers at 14 days plus a 10
percent reserve halves the write-off and still costs USD 57,605.53, USD 23,440.95 above
the blunt 15 percent reserve. Established sellers are 70 percent of the book, 89.7
percent of GMV and 61.6 percent of the write-off dollars, so exempting them gives up the
protection that costs the most and saves the float that costs the least.

The reserve curve has a knee at 15 percent. Each further five points adds about USD
3,440 of float to save under USD 2,000.

What I left out: fraud, KYC and account takeover. Holds are often imposed for those
reasons and this measures refund risk alone.
